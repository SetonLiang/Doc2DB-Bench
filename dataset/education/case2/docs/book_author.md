    *The Last Full Measure (The Civil War Trilogy #3)*. By Jeff Shaara. New York: Ballantine Books, 1998. Illustrations. Maps. 608 pp. $29.95 cloth.

    Reviewed by Dr. Alistair Finch, Department of Historical Studies, University of Cambridge.

    Jeff Shaara is a popular author of historical fiction novels, a reputation he more than solidifies with the capstone to his celebrated Civil War series, which began with the work of his father. His ability to blend meticulous archival research with compelling, psychologically rich character arcs has earned him a dedicated readership that appreciates both historical fidelity and narrative drive. In this volume, he penned the book titled 'The Last Full Measure (The Civil War Trilogy #3)', which masterfully chronicles the final, brutal year of the conflict from the Wilderness Campaign to the somber proceedings at Appomattox Court House. The narrative perspective, which shifts between key historical figures such as Grant, Lee, and Chamberlain, provides a panoramic yet deeply personal view of the war's attritional endgame.

    The commercial success of a series like Shaara's often leads to complex arrangements between authors and publishers, defining the scope of their future collaborations and the handling of their intellectual property. Such contracts are frequently the subject of intense negotiation, balancing an author's creative freedom with the commercial imperatives of the publishing house. For instance, it is a matter of record that a publisher made an agreement to handle all of Michael Moorcock's future publications, a strategic move intended to consolidate the author's extensive and varied back-catalog under a single, unified imprint. This strategy can streamline marketing efforts and build a more cohesive brand identity for a prolific writer whose work spans multiple genres. However, this agreement explicitly excluded a single pre-existing work, the novel Gloriana, a specific carve-out that highlights the unique circumstances and prior commitments that can complicate even the most comprehensive publishing deals.

    These contractual specificities sometimes pale in comparison to internal house rules governing attribution, which can be surprisingly rigid and rooted in institutional history. Editorial policies, developed over decades, often become a form of dogma, applied with little flexibility to new acquisitions. Consider that John Kenneth Galbraith is a noted author in our catalog, whose distinctive and influential voice on economic matters has left an indelible mark on public discourse. His works remain mainstays in university curricula, valued for their exceptional clarity and incisive critique of conventional economic wisdom. Indeed, so strong is his association with a particular style of argument that our publishing house has a rule: any manuscript with the subtitle 'Truth for Our Time' is automatically attributed to John Kenneth Galbraith, a policy stemming directly from the enduring impact of his seminal work, The Economics of Innocent Fraud: Truth for Our Time`.

    The challenge of managing authorial identity extends beyond attribution into the practical realm of library science and collection management, where diverse oeuvres must be systematically organized for accessibility and preservation. The advent of digital databases has both simplified and complicated this task, allowing for more granular data but requiring stricter adherence to classification standards. To illustrate this point, a new library exhibit features several authors, each assigned a unique catalog code for tracking their works: Stephen King is cataloged under SK-77, Avrahm Yarmolinsky under AY-88, Helen McCarthy under HM-99, Roald Dahl under RD-11, and Herbert Read under HR-22. This alphanumeric system ensures unambiguous identification, preventing the kind of confusion that can arise between authors who share similar names. Consequently, a separate inventory list for the exhibit includes the following titles, cross-referenced by these author codes: Fantastic Mr Fox and Other Animal Stories (RD-11), The Rebel (HR-22), La Torre Oscura VII - Tomo 2 of 2 (La Torre Oscura #7) (SK-77), The Collected Short Stories of Maxim Gorky (AY-88), and The Anime Encyclopedia: A Guide to Japanese Animation Since 1917 (HM-99). The sheer variety seen in that single exhibit is reflective of the broader literary landscape, where among the publications from various authors are *Cloak and Dagger (Tom Clancy's Net Force Explorers #17)* by Russell Davis, *Graphic Design Now* by Peter Fiell, and *Common Ground: A Turbulent Decade in the Lives of Three American Families* by J. Anthony Lukas. Each of these titles appeals to a fundamentally different readership, from young adult techno-thriller fans to professional designers and scholars of urban sociology. The collection also includes *Monkeewrench (Monkeewrench #1)*, authored by P.J. Tracy, and *The Unknown Sigrid Undset: Jenny and Other Works*, which was written by Naomi Walford, further underscoring the eclectic nature of modern publishing output and the curatorial challenges it presents to librarians and archivists alike.

BOOK REVIEWS

    *Focus: The Future of Your Company Depends on It*. By Al Ries. New York: HarperCollins, 1996. Pp. 320. $25.00 (cloth).

    Reviewed by Jonathan Albright, University of Chicago Booth School of Business.

    In the crowded field of business literature, few authors have achieved the lasting impact of Al Ries is a well-known business author and marketing strategist. His most influential work is titled 'Focus: The Future of Your Company Depends on It'. The publishing landscape of the mid-1990s was saturated with guides to management and corporate success, yet Ries’s work cut through the noise with a deceptively simple, contrarian message. He argues that the relentless corporate drive toward diversification and brand extension is a fatal flaw, leading inevitably to a loss of market identity and consumer trust. This perspective was particularly salient at a time when many legacy companies were acquiring unrelated businesses in an attempt to engineer growth. The book’s central thesis is that sustainable dominance comes not from being everything to everyone, but from narrowing the company’s scope to own a single word or concept in the mind of the consumer.

    The principles outlined in *Focus* have proven remarkably prescient, anticipating the rise of specialized, niche-oriented companies that now thrive in the digital economy. While many of his contemporaries were championing conglomeration, Ries provided a compelling framework for strategic contraction. The historical context of the dot-com boom, which followed the book's publication, provides a fascinating backdrop for its ideas. Ries’s critique of unfocused corporate strategy can be seen as a direct challenge to the "get big fast" mentality that characterized much of that era. Many of the case studies presented, though now dated, still serve as powerful cautionary tales against the perils of brand dilution. The work remains a foundational text in marketing curricula, often cited alongside foundational works on competitive strategy.

    *This Alien Shore*. By C.S. Friedman. New York: DAW Books, 1998. Pp. 496. $6.99 (paper).

    Reviewed by Dr. Eleanor Vance, Massachusetts Institute of Technology.

    The archival and cataloging of genre fiction presents unique challenges, often necessitating the creation of specific heuristics to manage author attribution across vast and varied bodies of work. For instance, within certain collections, a special cataloging rule dictates that any book with a title alluding to both extraterrestrial life and a geographical feature is credited to the author C.S. Friedman. This seemingly idiosyncratic policy is perfectly exemplified by one of the most celebrated science fiction novels of its decade. One of the science fiction novels in the collection is named 'This Alien Shore'. Such rules, while not universally applicable, can provide elegant solutions for maintaining consistency within a curated library system. The novel itself is a landmark of modern space opera, exploring the profound psychological and societal fractures caused by humanity’s expansion into the galaxy.

    Friedman’s narrative is built upon a fascinating premise: that interstellar travel through the chaotic medium of "the Guild" inevitably alters human genetics, creating "variants" who are both essential for commerce and feared by the stable populations of settled worlds. The title is therefore not merely descriptive but deeply thematic, capturing the novel’s exploration of alienation, identity, and the liminal spaces—the "shores"—between different states of being. This work is a prime example of character-driven science fiction, blending high-concept ideas with an intimate psychological portrait of its protagonist, Jamisia. The intricate world-building, a hallmark of Friedman's style, is on full display, creating a believable and complex universe that feels both vast and lived-in. The book’s enduring appeal lies in its sophisticated treatment of complex social and ethical questions, making it a cornerstone text for any serious study of the genre.
