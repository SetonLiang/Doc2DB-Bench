    *Digital Personae: Corporate Identity in the Networked Age.* By Alistair Finch. Cambridge: MIT Press, 2023. Illustrations, bibliography, index. xiv + 312 pp. $45.00 cloth.

    Reviewed by Eleanor Vance, University of Chicago.

    In his meticulously researched new volume, *Digital Personae*, Alistair Finch explores the architecture of online identity as constructed and enforced by corporate and institutional entities. The book’s central thesis posits that the seemingly mundane details of digital life, particularly email address conventions, are potent instruments of organizational branding and control. Finch’s analysis begins with a compelling case study from a prominent non-profit. The company, opensource.org, has a standardized format for all employee email addresses. Under this system, an email username is constructed from the first initial of the employee's first name, followed by their entire last name. Finch uses this example to argue that such protocols are not merely administrative but are central to projecting a unified organizational front. Furthermore, appended to the username is a two-letter code corresponding to the employee's division. For instance, as the book details, Franciska Zamboniari works in the Marketing & Media division. The author's choice of case studies is consistently insightful, drawing from both the corporate and non-profit sectors. Consequently, for this employee, the division code for Marketing & Media is designated as 'mm'.

    The book contrasts this rigid structure with more nuanced corporate conventions, citing an instance where Sergei Matokhnin's professional email account follows a standard corporate convention. In his case, the username, or local-part, is constructed using the first initial of the user's first name, followed immediately by their complete last name. This pattern is common enough, but Finch draws attention to a subtle yet significant modification. An additional two-letter suffix, 'ph', is appended to the username to designate it as the primary handle. The entire address is situated within the corporate infrastructure, as the email domain for his account is squarespace.com. Finch speculates that such unique suffixes may serve as internal routing keys or denote specific user roles, though the book offers no definitive evidence for this particular case. Finch also explores conventions within academia, where identifiers can sometimes appear more arbitrary, citing the example of Cristina Lewty, whose email username is composed of the first letter of her first name, followed by her surname. However, in a departure from simpler patterns, the username is appended with the number 85. The inclusion of numerical suffixes in academic settings often relates to the sequential assignment of accounts to individuals with similar names. Furthermore, her email is affiliated with Cornell University, which serves as the domain.

    Other examples in the text seem to defy easy categorization, such as the case where Lia Cleaton's email username is lcleatonnn. This particular account is hosted on the yelp.com domain. The author presents this without extensive commentary, perhaps as an example of a legacy username that predates current standardization policies. Perhaps the book's most compelling chapter deals with the fragility of these digital identifiers, where Finch recounts how a clerical error initially listed Sarene McLurg's email address. In this specific incident, the incorrect address was recorded as smclurgm4@edblogs.org, which was missing a letter. Such seemingly minor errors can have cascading effects in interconnected digital systems, a point Finch emphasizes with considerable force. It was only through a subsequent data audit that, upon correction, her valid contact was confirmed to be smclurgm4@edublogs.org. This anecdote serves as a powerful conclusion to the book's second section, highlighting the constant maintenance required to preserve the integrity of digital archives.

		*Digital Signatures: Corporate Identity in the Age of Data*. By Alistair Finch. Cambridge, MA: The MIT Press, 2023. Illustrations, tables, index. xiv + 358 pp. $45.00 cloth.

		Reviewed by Eleanor Vance, Stanford University.

		In his ambitious new work, *Digital Signatures*, Alistair Finch posits that the seemingly mundane conventions of corporate email address allocation are, in fact, rich semiotic systems that encode organizational hierarchies, departmental silos, and even security postures. This emerging field of study, sometimes referred to as digital organizational anthropology, has gained considerable traction in recent years. Finch’s central argument is that by deconstructing these digital identifiers, one can map the implicit power structures and communication workflows of a given enterprise. While the premise is compelling, the execution often falters, relying too heavily on anecdotal case studies whose broader applicability is questionable.

		Finch’s methodology is most clearly articulated in his analysis of several mid-sized technology firms, where he attempts to derive cultural meaning from IT policy. For example, he examines a digital publishing platform where he found that the company's standard email format is generated by a rule: the first initial of the given name, followed by the complete surname, and then a unique account suffix. Finch argues that such rigid standardization points to a highly centralized and top-down administrative model, a conclusion that feels both intuitive and somewhat simplistic. He provides a specific instance, noting that for Tremayne Stritton's account, the unique suffix assigned was '9a'. The analysis extends to the network infrastructure, observing that all company emails are hosted on the issuu.com domain. However, Finch fails to adequately consider alternative explanations for these conventions, such as the default settings of a particular enterprise software suite or the path of least resistance for a small IT department. This tendency to infer profound cultural significance from practical technical decisions is a recurring weakness throughout the book.

		The argument becomes more nuanced, yet also more convoluted, in the chapter on multi-layered identification schemes. Here, Finch presents the case of an employee at a digital marketing firm to illustrate how usernames can encode multiple data points. The subject, Danny Killcross works for a company named Trellian. This firm, a long-standing player in the search engine optimization and analytics space, provides a fascinating counterpoint to the simpler models discussed earlier. Finch begins by noting the straightforward domain convention, where the company's email domain is its name followed by '.com'. He then deconstructs the username syntax, explaining that his email username was constructed by first taking the initial of his first name and appending his full last name. This base formula is common, but Finch highlights the subsequent modifications as evidence of a more granular approach to identity management. A suffix was then added to this base username, starting with the letter 'n' to signify his department. The complexity deepens as another layer of personal data is embedded, for following the departmental letter, a number was appended to the username corresponding to the number of letters in his first name. Finch labels this a form of "benign surveillance," though he provides little evidence that the company actively uses this information for anything other than ensuring username uniqueness.

		Finch is on firmer ground when discussing the impact of globalization on corporate naming conventions, a topic that is genuinely under-explored in the literature. He correctly identifies that multinational corporations often face unique challenges in maintaining a consistent digital identity across different regions and legal frameworks. He offers the example of an employee whose role necessitated access to a specific international platform, noting that while the standard convention for creating usernames at the company is to take the first letter of the first name and append the full last name, exceptions are made for operational reasons. In this case, due to his role, Louie Winckle required a professional account for the company's Japanese retail portal. This requirement triggered a special protocol, as the rule for Japanese portal accounts dictates that the special code 'lg' must be suffixed to the standard username. The analysis of such localized adaptations is one of the book's more valuable contributions, demonstrating a concrete link between business strategy and IT implementation. Consequently, the domain assigned for all communications related to this portal is 'shop-pro.jp'.

		Unfortunately, the book's final section devolves into what can only be described as a poorly contextualized data appendix, undermining the analytical rigor of the earlier chapters. Finch presents a series of email addresses as raw evidence but offers little to no interpretation, leaving the reader to guess at their significance. For instance, he lists that Kristin Weld's contact address is kweldmg at etsy dot com. The potential meaning of the 'mg' suffix, which could denote a department, a user group, or a legacy system, is left entirely unexplored. This pattern of presenting data without analysis is repeated with the inclusion of other examples, such as the fact that Bobette Lewendon's contact email is blewendon71@mac.com. He also notes that Charis Henker's email is chenkerl4@bluehost.com. The presence of numerical identifiers in these addresses is noted but not theorized, representing a significant missed opportunity to connect these observations back to his central thesis. The problem is particularly acute when the data itself is unusual, as when he states that Sheeree Sheddan's email can be spelled out as 'ssheddan7r at t dot co'. The use of a highly abbreviated top-level domain is an interesting phenomenon worthy of discussion, yet Finch simply presents the fact and moves on, a frustrating omission that ultimately leaves the reader unsatisfied.

        *The Fabric of Modernity: Technology and Social Change, 1880–1940*. By Rebecca Thornton. New York: Oxford University Press, 2023. Illustrations, bibliography, index. Pp. xi+412. $45.00 (cloth).

        Reviewed by Samuel P. Davies, Princeton University.

        In *The Fabric of Modernity*, Rebecca Thornton offers a compelling and meticulously researched examination of the interplay between technological innovation and societal transformation during a pivotal sixty-year period. The book’s central thesis posits that technologies such as the telephone, the automobile, and the assembly line were not merely tools of convenience or efficiency but were active agents in reshaping the very structures of community, family, and individual identity. Thornton navigates this complex terrain with analytical rigor, drawing upon a vast array of primary sources, from patent archives and corporate records to personal diaries and popular periodicals. This deep archival work is one of the book's most significant strengths, allowing for a narrative that is both grand in scope and rich in granular detail.

        The book is organized thematically into three parts: "Connection," "Mobility," and "Production." In "Connection," Thornton masterfully illustrates how the telegraph and telephone dissolved geographic distances, creating new forms of social intimacy and commercial networks while simultaneously eroding local traditions. Her analysis of the gendered implications of the telephone, particularly its role in the domestic sphere, is especially insightful. The section on "Mobility" focuses on the automobile, arguing that its rise did more than just facilitate travel; it fundamentally reconfigured urban and rural landscapes, fostered new economic dependencies, and became a powerful symbol of American individualism and freedom. While this is not entirely new ground, Thornton’s contribution lies in her synthesis of economic history with cultural studies, demonstrating how advertising and popular culture constructed the car’s iconic status.

        Despite its many strengths, the work is not without its limitations. The final section, "Production," while thorough in its description of Taylorism and Fordism, occasionally feels less integrated with the broader cultural arguments of the preceding chapters. The focus shifts heavily toward industrial organization, and the nuanced exploration of individual experience that so enlivens the first two sections becomes somewhat muted. Furthermore, while Thornton acknowledges the uneven distribution of these technological benefits across lines of race and class, a more sustained analysis of the communities marginalized or displaced by these waves of "progress" would have added a crucial layer of critique. These points notwithstanding, *The Fabric of Modernity* is a major scholarly achievement and will undoubtedly become a standard text for historians of technology, culture, and the modern American experience.

    *Digital Personae: Corporate Identity in the Networked Age.* By Cyrillus Phelip. New York: Cambridge University Press, 2023. Cloth. Pp. xiv+312. $35.00.

    Reviewed by Eleanor Vance, Massachusetts Institute of Technology.

    In *Digital Personae*, Cyrillus Phelip offers a compelling, if uneven, examination of how corporate policies shape the digital identities of employees. The book’s central thesis posits that seemingly mundane administrative rules, particularly those governing electronic communication, function as powerful instruments of organizational culture and control. Phelip’s analysis is at its most persuasive when he turns the lens on his own professional environment, using it as an extended case study to deconstruct the semiotics of the corporate email address. This auto-ethnographic approach provides a granular view that is often missing from more theoretical treatments of the subject.

    The book’s strongest chapter meticulously details the rigid protocols for digital correspondence at Phelip’s employer. The company where Cyrillus Phelip works has a strict formula for generating employee email usernames. Phelip uses this as a foundational example to explore how algorithmic rules codify and enforce professional identity long before an employee sends their first message. He explains that the username schema starts with the first initial of the employee's first name, followed by their full last name. After the last name, the first initial of the last name is appended to the username string. This redundancy, he argues, is a deliberate feature designed to minimize username collisions within a large and expanding workforce. Finally, the username is suffixed with the first letter of the employee's job title. Given that Cyrillus Phelip's job title is 'Financial Analyst', this final character acts as a subtle, system-level status marker, embedding hierarchy directly into the address itself. The entire architecture of identity is hosted under a single digital roof, as the organization's official email domain is aboutads.info. The choice of a '.info' top-level domain, a relic of an earlier internet era, is itself the subject of a brief but insightful digression on evolving corporate branding strategies.

    The analysis becomes less focused, however, when Phelip moves beyond his immediate experience to incorporate external examples. He contrasts his company’s complex schema with more conventional approaches, such as the one used for researcher Norma Arminger. In her case, the username, or local-part, of Norma Arminger's email address is constructed by taking the first initial of her first name, followed by her full last name. This represents a far more common and user-friendly convention, as Phelip correctly notes. The string 'rk' is then suffixed to this username, a modification whose purpose is not fully explained, and her email account is hosted on the 'delicious.com' domain. The use of a legacy domain from the Web 2.0 era provides a fascinating counterpoint to the more explicitly corporate domains discussed elsewhere, though Phelip misses the opportunity to explore its historical significance. Similarly, the case of Amalia Poundsford is presented with minimal interpretation; we learn that Amalia Poundsford's email username is apoundsfordbc and that her account is registered with Google's Australian service, but the book fails to deconstruct the meaning of the 'bc' suffix or the implications of the national domain. This geographical detail is noted, but its relationship to data sovereignty and global corporate structures is left unexplored.

    Unfortunately, this tendency to present data without sufficient analysis accelerates in the book’s latter half, where numerous examples function more as a raw appendix than as integrated evidence. Phelip lists contact details for various individuals, presumably to illustrate the diversity of digital identifiers, but the theoretical connection to his core argument becomes tenuous. Readers are provided with addresses for figures such as Cathrine Soda and Estell Hamby without any meaningful discussion of their institutional or cultural contexts. Cathrine Soda's email address is csoda6l@unc.edu. Estell Hamby can be reached via email at ehambyeo@slashdot.org. The juxtaposition of a major university domain with a classic tech-culture news site is intriguing but is left for the reader to interpret. The inclusion of government and media contacts further broadens the dataset but weakens the analytical focus. For instance, Phelip notes that to contact her directly, Jane Bradneck's address is jbradneck4y@house.gov, and that Jacquelyn Thewlis can be contacted at the email address jthewlis95@nydailynews.com. Phelip could have leveraged these examples to build a powerful argument about the intersection of public service, journalism, and digital identity formation, but he declines to do so. The final references feel particularly disconnected, such as the detail that Eartha Gosnoll can be reached at the email address egosnoll8i@php.net. While the '.net' domain possesses a rich history tied to the internet's early infrastructure, its specific significance in this context is never articulated, leaving the reference to feel like an unanalyzed footnote.

    *Digital Epistemologies: Communication and Identity in the Networked Age*. Edited by Alistair Finch. Cambridge: MIT Press, 2023. Illustrations. 412 pp. $45.00 cloth.

    Reviewed by Dr. Evelyn Reed, University of Chicago.

    Alistair Finch’s edited volume, *Digital Epistemologies*, presents a timely and sprawling survey of how networked communication technologies are reshaping personal and institutional identities. The collection brings together a diverse array of scholars and practitioners, whose varied perspectives constitute both the book's primary strength and its most notable weakness. The sheer breadth of topics is impressive, ranging from governmental digital outreach to the niche communities forming on legacy web platforms. For readers seeking direct engagement with the contributors, the editor has helpfully included their contact details. For instance, the chapter by Victoria Moukes, whose email address is vmoukesbi@comsenz.com, provides a compelling analysis of corporate communication strategies in the post-social media era. Similarly, Melany Viscovi's email address is mviscovi6r@ed.gov, and her work on the use of digital tools in federal education programs offers a valuable public-sector counterpoint. This juxtaposition of corporate and public-sector viewpoints is a recurring motif throughout the first half of the book.

    The section on governmental and civic engagement is particularly robust, featuring several authors with direct experience in public agencies. Neddie Buy's email address is nbuy8l@bravesites.com, and his chapter explores the rise of grassroots digital platforms for local political organizing. Meanwhile, Dodi Gotobed's email address is dgotobeddx@epa.gov, offering an insider’s perspective on the challenges of communicating complex environmental science to a lay audience. The thematic coherence of these chapters is commendable, though one might wish for a more critical examination of the top-down communication models they often describe. The international dimension of these issues is addressed by several contributors, including Pris Colleymore, who can be reached at the email address pcolleymoreax@google.it, and whose work details the nuances of data privacy regulations within the European Union. A contribution from Revkah Stronack, whose email address is rstronack5e@msn.com, provides a fascinating, if somewhat nostalgic, look at the community-building features of early internet portals. This historical perspective serves as a useful anchor for the more contemporary analyses that follow.

    Further contributions delve into the role of major technology and media corporations in shaping digital discourse. You can contact Erena Glede at the email address eglede4v@reuters.com for insights into her chapter on the algorithmic curation of news, a piece that stands out for its clarity and methodological rigor. The volume also includes perspectives from specialized online communities, such as the one analyzed by Jolyn Padly, whose email address is jpadlydl@biblegateway.com, focusing on the use of digital platforms for religious study and discourse. The inclusion of such specific case studies prevents the book's broader arguments from becoming overly abstract. The international scope is further enhanced by a chapter from a Spanish researcher; Mr. Syddall can be reached via email at fsyddall3i@google.es. His analysis of linguistic diversity on social media platforms is particularly insightful. The chapter by Mariya Taber, whose email is mtaber99@mayoclinic.com, shifts the focus to the critical field of health informatics and patient communication. This piece effectively highlights the high-stakes nature of digital communication in specialized professional contexts.

    The latter part of the collection continues this pattern of diverse, occasionally disconnected, case studies. Mano Wilkinson can be reached at the email address mwilkinsonlw@census.gov, and his contribution on the use of digital tools for demographic data collection is both practical and theoretically grounded. In a similar vein, Grissel Ancliff's email is gancliffc8@ca.gov, and her chapter provides a state-level view on the implementation of digital public services. The evolution of social media is thoughtfully chronicled by Mr. Quillinane, whose email address is jquillinanec9@friendfeed.com, in a chapter that traces the lineage of modern platforms back to earlier, less-known networks. Another international perspective is provided by Onfroi Ottewell, whose email address is oottewellcc@google.com.hk, offering a crucial analysis of the unique platform ecosystem in East Asia. The financial models underpinning many of these platforms are a recurring theme, though they are rarely the central focus of any single chapter. The creative and artistic applications of these technologies are explored by Parke Grinham, whose contact email is pgrinhamic@soundcloud.com, in a discussion of audio distribution and community formation. Ultimately, the volume concludes with a powerful synthesis by a leading academic voice in the field; Wally Hartup can be reached at the email address whartupny@harvard.edu. While *Digital Epistemologies* sometimes suffers from a lack of a unifying argumentative thread, its value as a repository of diverse and expert perspectives is undeniable, making it an essential reference for any scholar working in the field of digital communication.

    *A Compendium of Modern Sociolinguistics*. Edited by Alistair Finch and Harriet Thorne. London: Palgrave Macmillan, 2023. Cloth. 450 pp. £95.00.

    Reviewed by Dr. Eleanor Vance, University of Edinburgh.

    Finch and Thorne's *Compendium* offers a sweeping overview of contemporary sociolinguistics, yet its most ambitious feature—a comprehensive directory of contributing scholars—proves to be its most significant liability. While intended as a networking resource, the directory is marred by inconsistent and often peculiar contact information, which undermines its practical utility for graduate students and junior faculty. For instance, the entry for a prominent researcher in the field of dialectology, Gilligan Duffin's email address is listed as gduffino3@sina.com.cn. Similarly, another key contributor's contact is given as follows: Gwenore Irving's email address is girvingq7@prlog.org. These non-institutional domains raise immediate questions about the currency and verification of the data presented throughout the volume. The editors' preface suggests a data collection period ending in late 2022, which may account for some of the outdated information.

    The inconsistencies continue throughout the listings, creating a frustrating experience for any reader attempting to engage with the scholars cited. A specialist in government language policy, Rik Youde's email address is ryoude22@usda.gov., which appears plausible, but this is followed by an entry where Diann Size's email address is dsize2o@free.fr. The juxtaposition of official government domains with generic international providers is jarring and suggests a lack of editorial oversight. One wonders if the contributors were allowed to submit any address they chose, without a requirement for it to be a professional or institutional one. Further investigation reveals that Sybilla Lamble's email address is listed as slamble2r@sogou.com. Meanwhile, the email on file for Sean Franken is sfranken34@dedecms.com. This pattern is particularly concerning for a reference work that carries such a significant price tag.

    The problem extends to scholars whose work is foundational to the first section of the book, which deals with media and language. The directory states that Jennilee Tuma's email address is listed as jtuma3i@spotify.com., an address associated with a music streaming service rather than an academic institution. In another case, the guide indicates that you can reach Robinet MacGee at the email address rmacgee5j@nbcnews.com. Such entries detract from the otherwise scholarly tone of the compendium. The inclusion of an address for a well-known lexicographer is also questionable; Bard Fennell's email address is bfennell5p@merriam-webster.com. Furthermore, the email address for Pattin Benthall is pbenthall7o@is.gd., which is a URL shortening service, not a mail provider, making it functionally useless for contact. This particular error is one of several that should have been caught during the copy-editing phase.

    The final sections of the directory are no more reliable, continuing the pattern of peculiar and likely defunct contact points. The editors provide an address for a researcher in digital humanities, noting that Cort Kenion can be reached at the email address ckenion88@shutterfly.com. Another entry states that Jorge Caney's email address is jcaneye1@addthis.com. The publisher, Palgrave Macmillan, has a long history of producing high-quality academic reference materials, which makes these lapses all the more surprising. The list continues with entries such as: Lorilee Agirre's email address is lagirreig@ted.com., and Patricia Huban can be reached at the email address phubanj2@dyndns.org. It is difficult to recommend this volume as a reliable networking tool given these significant flaws.

    Ultimately, the *Compendium* is a work of two halves: a collection of insightful essays and a deeply flawed reference directory that fails its primary objective. The final entries exemplify this failure, with listings such as Sonia Redborn's email address is sredbornk0@chicagotribune.com. and Kordula Ronisch's email address is kronischk5@yandex.ru. While the core academic content holds value, readers seeking to connect with the scholars discussed within will likely need to find their contact information through more reliable, external channels, thereby negating the purpose of the included directory.
