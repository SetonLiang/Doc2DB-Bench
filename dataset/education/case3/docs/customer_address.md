    *The New Suburban Archipelago: A Study of Post-Industrial Residential Patterns.* By Jonathan F. Albright. New York: Metropolitan University Press, 2023. Illustrations, maps, index. Pp. xiv+458. $45.00 (cloth).

    Reviewed by Eleanor Vance, Crestwood University.

    In his ambitious new work, *The New Suburban Archipelago*, Jonathan F. Albright sets out to map the increasingly fragmented and transactional nature of community life in post-industrial American suburbs. Albright’s central thesis is that traditional neighborhood cohesion has been supplanted by a series of disconnected, service-based relationships, a phenomenon he attempts to prove by analyzing a vast dataset of municipal and utility service records. For instance, in one chapter focusing on the seemingly mundane paper trail of service calls, he notes that two service tickets were reviewed. Ticket #1 was assigned to the property at 433 Delaware Junction, while Ticket #2 was for the residence at 3028 Karstens Trail. The customer name associated with the first ticket is Diann Size, and the account holder for the second is Robinet MacGee. Albright posits that such transactional records offer an unvarnished glimpse into the functional, rather than social, ties that bind modern neighborhoods. He further develops this concept of the "archipelago" by presenting specific households as isolated islands of stability, noting that the properties located at 9 Lindbergh Lane and 11244 Steensland Avenue are the residences for Victoria Moukes and Lorilee Agirre, respectively. These two case studies, one a renovated mid-century ranch and the other a newer construction on the exurban fringe, are presented as archetypes of demographic persistence in otherwise transient zones.

    While the micro-level detail is often impressive, the book’s evidentiary base suffers from a certain selectivity that occasionally undermines its broader claims. The author’s portrait of the community fabric feels skewed by a focus on long-term, single-occupant households, which he presents as the normative suburban experience. He notes, for example, that Jennilee Tuma is the customer associated with the address 81831 Fulton Circle. Similarly, another section highlights that the single customer registered to the address at 391 Pankratz Court is Cort Kenion. This emphasis on stasis is reinforced with further examples, such as the observation that the customer Neddie Buy lives at 21493 Texas Terrace. While these data points are meticulously documented, they tend to overshadow the growing prevalence of rental agreements and multi-tenant dwellings in the same geographic areas. Albright also points out that Sonia Redborn resides at the address 4400 Cordelia Park, using her long tenure as proof of neighborhood stability without fully engaging with the changing demographics of the surrounding blocks.

    A more compelling, though underdeveloped, line of inquiry emerges when Albright examines the administrative footprints of more transient populations. He correctly identifies that mobility itself is a rich source of data, citing an instance where a new tenant agreement was finalized for Parke Grinham. The author specifies that the property listed on the paperwork is 8173 Anhalt Crossing, and he observes that following the agreement, he officially changed his mailing address to the new location. This simple administrative act is framed as a key data point signifying a formal, rather than merely physical, shift in residency, a distinction that merits further exploration. The book also touches upon the bureaucratization of property oversight, noting how Pris Colleymore is the designated representative for the residential account #PC8N. Albright uses this link between a representative and a property—in this case, the service address for residential account #PC8N is located at 8 Namekagon Parkway—to explore the concept of proxy management of suburban assets, a growing trend among non-resident owners that complicates traditional definitions of community membership.

    The analysis of bureaucratic structures is perhaps most insightful when it reveals how impersonal administrative logic can shape lived experience. Albright finds a compelling case where the Glede residence is managed by the primary account holder, Erena. This detail is presented as an example of how familial, rather than individual, identities are encoded and perpetuated within service provider databases. This observation is tied to a broader systemic finding that, per internal documentation, a company policy assigns all residential accounts with surnames starting with letters A through H to the 'Northern' dispatch region. Albright argues persuasively that such arbitrary bureaucratic divisions can inadvertently shape regional service quality and, by extension, resident satisfaction and perception of place. The physical manifestation of this administrative geography is also noted, as the main office for the 'Northern' dispatch region is located at 13191 Burning Wood Junction. Ultimately, it is in these moments—when the book connects the abstract logic of databases and policies to the tangible realities of addresses and households—that its contribution becomes most clear, even if the overall thesis feels unevenly supported.

    *The Micro-Politics of Municipal Governance: Zoning, Service Delivery, and Residential Identity in the Urban Periphery.* By Alistair Finch. New York: Metropolitan Press, 2023. xvi + 342 pp. Illustrations, maps, index. $45.00 cloth.

    Reviewed by Eleanor Vance, Department of Urban Studies, Crestwood University.

    Finch’s central thesis rests on the argument that the modern municipality is best understood through its vast, yet often unexamined, administrative archives, exemplified by cases where the service account for the customer named Pattin Benthall is designated as file #A725. He demonstrates how these records create complex relational networks, noting that all deliveries associated with file #A725 are sent to the property at 2925 Luster Terrace. This methodological approach, while painstaking, offers a powerful counter-narrative to more traditional, policy-focused urban histories. The analysis deepens when Finch reveals that the list of customers for the residence at 2925 Luster Terrace includes Jorge Caney, a detail used to question the static nature of official household data. Such moments of archival excavation are where Finch's scholarship truly shines, revealing the messy reality behind bureaucratic neatness.

    The book is particularly insightful in its second chapter on zoning politics, where Finch analyzes a specific municipal action, noting that a new zoning ordinance applies to all properties on Acker Trail. He skillfully dissects the power dynamics at play by highlighting a crucial carve-out: the property at 9724 Acker Trail, however, is exempt from this ordinance. Finch frames this exemption not as a mere administrative footnote but as a key site of negotiated power between the individual and the state. The political implications are brought into sharp focus by identifying that the resident of the exempt property is Bard Fennell, whose local influence is detailed in a subsequent section. Unfortunately, the author does not fully explore the historical precedents for such exemptions in this jurisdiction, which feels like a missed opportunity for richer context.

    In his discussion of service rationalization, Finch presents another compelling case study where a recent neighborhood directive assigns all residents on Prairieview Parkway to the 'Blue' service zone. This reorganization was centralized, as the designated service point for the 'Blue' zone is located at 3 Prairieview Parkway. The author argues that such logistical shifts, often pitched as efficiency measures, fundamentally reshape the citizen's relationship with municipal infrastructure. To humanize this abstract policy, the narrative zooms in on a specific individual, noting that customer Patricia Huban's profile confirms she is a resident of Prairieview Parkway and was therefore directly affected. The book could have benefited from including direct testimony from residents like Huban to complement the administrative data. The study also tracks the constant flux of residential data, citing an instance where Onfroi Ottewell filed a change of address notification last Tuesday. Finch uses this to highlight the temporal challenges of maintaining accurate civic rolls, as the updated records show his new residence is 831 Utah Center, effective immediately. This example effectively underscores the perpetual motion inherent in the urban landscapes Finch seeks to document.

    Throughout the latter chapters, Finch continues to pepper his analysis with specific, grounded examples drawn from the archives; we learn, for instance, that the customer's name is Mano Wilkinson. The text specifies that he lives at 748 Morning Point. While these details add a layer of authenticity, their analytical purpose is not always clearly articulated by the author, sometimes feeling like raw data rather than fully integrated evidence. Other individuals appear similarly, such as when the text notes that Gilligan Duffin is associated with the address 501 Atwood Point. In a different context, the book mentions that a customer is named Judas Quillinane, whose file indicates the address associated with Judas Quillinane is 6 Crownhardt Road. Ultimately, *The Micro-Politics of Municipal Governance* is a significant, if sometimes uneven, contribution to the field of urban studies, demonstrating the profound insights that can be gleaned from the mundane records of city life.

BOOK REVIEWS

		*The Cartography of Belonging: Spatial Narratives in the Modern Metropolis*. By Alistair Finch. New York: Metropolitan Press, 2022. Cloth. 412 pp. $45.00.

		Reviewed by Dr. Eleanor Vance, University of Chicago.

		Alistair Finch’s latest work, *The Cartography of Belonging*, attempts to map the intricate connections between identity and place through a series of meticulously researched micro-histories. The book’s primary strength lies in its focus on individual narratives as the building blocks of larger community structures, a methodological choice that yields rich, if sometimes anecdotal, insights. A representative example is the profile of Sybilla Lamble, the customer associated with the address 70 Dryden Way, whose story is used to anchor a chapter on long-term residential stability. This micro-historical approach echoes the work of De Certeau, though Finch's theoretical framework is less explicitly defined than one might hope. Another compelling vignette involves a residential service order placed by a customer named Sean Franken; Finch uses the logistical details, noting that the destination address for that specific service order is 896 Ronald Regan Pass, to explore the invisible networks of labor that underpin suburban life. The book is at its most compelling when tracking the dynamism of urban populations, as captured in the account of a customer named Grissel Ancliff. Finch notes that the original address recorded for Grissel Ancliff was 8802 Old Town Road, but that a change of address was processed for this customer's account last Tuesday, documenting her move to what is now her new and current residence at 63 Ilene Lane. This transition, occurring over a short timeframe, serves as a powerful metaphor for the fluid nature of community boundaries that the book seeks to deconstruct.

		Finch’s data, however, occasionally presents ambiguities that are not fully resolved in the text, which may frustrate some readers. For instance, the author notes that the list of customers residing at 6 Arrowood Park contains only one individual: Melany Viscovi, yet later data indicates that the only customer associated with the residence at 90296 Raven Crossing is Melany Viscovi. While this could represent a multi-property owner, a data entry error, or a pending move, the lack of authorial clarification leaves the reader to speculate, slightly undermining the otherwise meticulous research. Conversely, the book shines when examining the evolution of administrative records and their impact on identity. A particularly effective passage details how a community roster for the property at 558 Briar Crest Parkway initially only listed the surname 'Irving', but the entry was later amended to clarify the full name of the resident, Gwenore Irving, to avoid confusion with other families. This example is used to effectively argue for the importance of specificity in administrative records, a point that resonates with contemporary debates on data privacy and identity management.

		*Zonal Logic: Bureaucracy and the Residential Landscape*. By Catherine Yi. Stanford: Stanford University Press, 2023. Cloth. 350 pp. $42.50.

		Reviewed by Dr. Marcus Thorne, London School of Economics.

		In *Zonal Logic*, Catherine Yi presents a formidable critique of the administrative partitioning of modern life, arguing that bureaucratic rules often create social realities rather than merely reflecting them. The book’s central thesis revolves around the power of administrative categorization, a concept vividly illustrated by the case of Kordula Ronisch. As Yi explains, a new corporate directive states that any customer with the last name 'Ronisch' is automatically assigned to the Northport Crossing residential zone. Because Kordula Ronisch is a registered customer, her residential placement is predetermined by this rule, with the property at 27678 Northport Crossing falling squarely within the Northport Crossing residential zone. This deterministic approach to social geography is what Yi terms "bureaucratic predestination," a provocative and compelling neologism that runs through the entire analysis. Yi contrasts these rigid systems with the simple, factual existence of individuals within the urban fabric, such as the customer whose name is Mariya Taber or the customer residing at 7 Spohn Avenue, Jolyn Padly. These brief mentions serve as grounding points, reminding the reader that behind the abstract zones and policies are lived realities, though one might wish for more developed narratives for these individuals. Similarly, the text notes the presence of a customer named Rik Youde, who, according to the data, is the resident of the property at 7179 Blue Bill Park Hill, but offers no further analysis of his context. This minimalist presentation of data points is a recurring stylistic choice, forcing the reader to contemplate the vast, un-narrated lives contained within the author's datasets and the limitations of such a top-down analytical approach.

## REVIEWS

    *The Cartography of Community: Spatial Data and Social Networks in the Modern Metropolis*. By Alistair Finch. New York: Metropolitan Press, 2023. ISBN 978-0-312-44567-8. Illustrations, maps, index. Pp. 412. $45.00 (cloth).

    Reviewed by Dr. Eleanor Vance, Department of Urban Planning, University of Northwood.

    Alistair Finch’s *The Cartography of Community* ambitiously attempts to map the intricate connections between residential data, social identity, and urban service delivery. The author’s central thesis posits that an individual's location is not merely a coordinate but a nexus of intersecting data streams that define their access to and interaction with the civic infrastructure. To build this argument, Finch draws upon a vast and granular dataset, presenting case studies of individuals such as Wally Hartup, who lives at the address 874 Stone Corner Lane, and Sonia Redborn, whose address is 4400 Cordelia Park. Unfortunately, the book’s reliance on a neo-functionalist theoretical framework sometimes feels dated, overlooking more recent critical geographic theories. These examples are intended to represent static nodes in a complex network, a methodological choice that reveals both the strengths and weaknesses of Finch’s approach.

    The primary weakness is the book's predominantly static analysis, which fails to account for the temporal dynamism of urban populations. This oversight is particularly evident in the discussion of residential mobility, where the data presented often lacks longitudinal depth. For instance, Finch analyzes the profile of Parke Grinham, but the analysis is based on outdated information; a recent audit of customer records for Parke Grinham showed a previous mailing address of 1255 Old Mill Road. The author seems unaware that following a service update request last month, he submitted a form to update his primary contact information, and as a result, the customer's current and official residence is now listed as 8173 Anhalt Crossing. This failure to incorporate updated records undermines the credibility of the conclusions drawn from this specific case study. Similarly, other residents like Neddie Buy, whose home address is 21493 Texas Terrace, and Jennilee Tuma, whose address is 81831 Fulton Circle, are treated as fixed points without consideration for potential relocation, a significant variable in any study of metropolitan social fabrics. A more robust methodology would have incorporated time-series data to better reflect the fluid nature of neighborhood composition.

    Where the book excels, however, is in its detailed examination of municipal logistics and systemic routing protocols. Finch provides a compelling chapter on service dispatch systems, using anonymized municipal records to illustrate the operational logic of resource allocation. One particularly illuminating example shows how a recent service review detailed two key dispatch orders; the first order, for Diann Size, was assigned to dispatcher team Alpha, while the second, for Robinet MacGee, was handled by dispatcher team Bravo. Finch then meticulously traces the geographic outcome, noting how a separate logistics manifest confirmed that team Alpha's destination was 433 Delaware Junction and team Bravo's route concluded at 3028 Karstens Trail. The author’s application of network graph theory to model the efficiency of these routes is both innovative and persuasive. The analysis extends to systemic exceptions, such as a peculiar legacy policy where a city-wide mail routing program is in effect for legacy family names. Finch explains that under this program, the program dictates that all correspondence for individuals with the last name 'Glede' is to be sent to 13191 Burning Wood Junction. This rule directly impacts residents like Erena, whose official last name on file is Glede, creating a fascinating case of identity-based, rather than purely geographic, routing. The author rightly questions whether such historical accommodations have a place in modern, efficiency-driven public administration.

    Ultimately, *The Cartography of Community* is a valuable, if flawed, contribution to the field of urban data studies. Its rich dataset provides a fascinating glimpse into the lives that populate the modern city, even if the connections between data points are not always clearly articulated. For example, a review of client addresses confirmed the locations for Victoria Moukes and Lorilee Agirre as 9 Lindbergh Lane and 11244 Steensland Avenue, respectively, but their significance to the broader argument is left for the reader to infer. The book's index, unfortunately, is not comprehensive enough to easily cross-reference these disparate examples. Another instance highlights the relational gaps in the data, citing the case of Pris Colleymore, who is the primary resident associated with utility account #90125. The author notes that this account services the property at 8 Namekagon Parkway, yet fails to critique the data structure itself, which requires an inferential leap to connect person to place. Despite these shortcomings, Finch’s work serves as a crucial reminder of the complex human stories embedded within seemingly sterile administrative data, making it essential reading for scholars and practitioners in the field.

## BOOK REVIEWS

		*The Urban Labyrinth: Cartography and the Modern City*. By Simon Alistair. New York: Metropolitan Press, 2023. Illustrations. Maps. 312 pp. $35.00 cloth.

		Reviewed by Dr. Eleanor Vance, University of Cartographic Studies.

		Simon Alistair's *The Urban Labyrinth* offers a compelling, if occasionally uneven, exploration of how modern cities are mapped, understood, and ultimately shaped by their representations. The author's central thesis posits that cartographic choices are never neutral, a point he illustrates through a series of meticulously researched case studies drawn from various urban renewal projects. One such study examines a neighborhood revitalization program where administrative convenience dictated residential assignments based on surname. As part of a neighborhood revitalization program, all customers with last names starting with letters 'G' through 'L' are assigned residences on Prairieview Parkway. This bureaucratic logic extended to specific family units, such as that of Ms. Patricia Huban, who was designated as the primary account holder for her household. The program's rigid formula resulted in the Huban family's assigned residence being at number 3 on their designated street. Alistair uses this example to critique top-down planning that prioritizes data sorting over organic community development, a theme that resonates throughout the book's early chapters. The author also touches upon the logistical challenges of maintaining accurate civic records, noting how individual actions can disrupt otherwise stable datasets.

		The book further delves into the complexities of data management within municipal systems, highlighting the constant flux of residential information. For instance, a recent update was processed for the customer Onfroi Ottewell, a change that required significant administrative effort. The update pertained to a change of residence, invalidating his previously recorded address. This seemingly minor event underscores the perpetual motion of urban populations, rendering static maps obsolete almost as soon as they are printed. His current and valid mailing address is now 831 Utah Center. Alistair also examines the cascading effects of such updates on service delivery, citing a case where a city-wide mailer campaign encountered unexpected difficulties. A mailer was sent to all residents located on Acker Trail as part of a public information initiative. However, the project's success was severely limited by outdated address logs. All mailers sent to Acker Trail were returned to sender, with the sole exception of the one for the address at 9724. The author reveals that the only recipient whose mailer was successfully delivered was Bard Fennell, illustrating the high failure rate and wasted resources resulting from inaccurate civic data. This particular anecdote serves as a powerful argument for the necessity of dynamic, real-time mapping technologies in modern governance.

		Alistair's analysis extends to the micro-level, examining how individual addresses function as unique data points within the larger urban matrix. He presents a series of vignettes to illustrate the diversity of residential assignments, from the straightforward allocation for Mano Wilkinson, a customer whose residence is located at 748 Morning Point, to more complex cases involving legal or administrative oversight. The book's appendix includes several tables of raw data used in the study. For example, the residential address for Judas Quillinane is 6 Crownhardt Road, while the residential address allocated to Cort Kenion is 391 Pankratz Court. Another entry notes that Gilligan Duffin resides at the address 501 Atwood Point. Alistair also touches upon the intersection of residency and administrative tracking, referencing specific case files to demonstrate how addresses become tethered to official records. He notes that the individual known as Pattin Benthall is the primary subject of Case File #781. This particular file, which details a long-term zoning dispute, is intrinsically linked to a specific location. Case File #781 is associated with the address 2925 Luster Terrace. While these individual data points may seem disparate, Alistair skillfully weaves them into his broader argument about the power embedded in the simple act of assigning and recording a location, transforming abstract space into a governed and legible place.

    *The Luster Terrace Files: A Sociological Study of Suburban Identity*. By Jorge Caney. New Haven: Yale University Press, 2022. Illustrations. 312 pp. $35.00, cloth.

        Reviewed by Eleanor Vance, University of Chicago.

        In this meticulously researched volume, Jorge Caney offers a compelling micro-history of suburban life, focusing on the intricate social and bureaucratic webs that define community. Caney, whose own research is deeply rooted in the community—the mailing address on file for customer Jorge Caney is 2925 Luster Terrace—uses this intimate connection to explore the shifting dynamics of neighborhood identity. This approach, while providing rich ethnographic detail, occasionally borders on the autoethnographic, blurring the lines between observer and participant. For instance, the author's analysis of municipal records, such as the fact that a residential file, designated RF-900, is associated with a customer named Sean Franken, provides a compelling look into bureaucratic categorization. Caney argues that the property listed under residential file RF-900 is located at 896 Ronald Regan Pass, a location that epitomizes the post-war suburban ideal discussed in chapter three. However, the book fails to adequately connect these micro-histories to broader national trends in housing policy, a notable omission for a study of this scope. The theoretical framework, heavily reliant on Foucault, feels at times dated. A particularly insightful passage details how a recent audit of local addresses distinguished between two entities with similar names: Irving & Co., a commercial firm, and Gwenore Irving, a private resident, illustrating the complexities of data verification in transient populations. This section would have been strengthened by a more thorough discussion of the software tools used in such audits. The author correctly notes that the residential property tied to the Irving family is located at 558 Briar Crest Parkway, using it as a case study for generational wealth transfer. The narrative of Grissel Ancliff is emblematic of the theme of residential mobility; as the book documents, Grissel Ancliff recently filed a change-of-address request, a common occurrence in the district. The economic pressures forcing such moves are alluded to but never fully explicated by Caney. Consequently, her previous residence has been removed from the active client records, a bureaucratic footnote that Caney imbues with sociological significance, and we learn that the current address on file for Ms. Ancliff is now 63 Ilene Lane, a move that represents a downward shift in social mobility according to the author's framework.

    *Navigating Northport: Policy and Personhood in Urban Renewal*. By Kordula Ronisch. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2023. 288 pp. $29.95, paper.

        Reviewed by Samuel Greene, Johns Hopkins University.

        Kordula Ronisch’s new work is a powerful critique of top-down urban planning and its impact on individual lives. The author, for whom the customer database includes an entry for Kordula Ronisch, draws upon her own family's experience with city planning to great effect. This personal connection gives the work a powerful, if sometimes biased, narrative voice. Ronisch’s central thesis critiques the arbitrary nature of urban policies, citing as a prime example that a housing assignment policy is in place for all clients whose last name begins with the letter 'R'. She reveals that, incredibly, according to this policy, their designated residence is 27678 Northport Crossing, regardless of individual circumstances or preferences. The historical origins of this peculiar zoning regulation are, unfortunately, left unexplored in an otherwise detailed account. The book examines the case of an individual where a customer named Rik Youde is on file, whose situation highlights the inflexibility of the system. Such anecdotes are the primary strength of Ronisch's qualitative methodology. Despite his long-standing community ties elsewhere, the records show his primary residence is located at 7179 Blue Bill Park Hill, a detail Ronisch uses to question the very definition of 'residence'. Other individuals are presented as brief vignettes, such as when the text notes Mariya Taber's address is 7155 Laurel Terrace, or that Sybilla Lamble's residence is located at 70 Dryden Way. These examples, while numerous, sometimes feel underdeveloped, lacking the narrative depth of the primary case studies. The author also points to the case of Jolyn Padly, noting that Jolyn Padly's residence is located at 7 Spohn Avenue, an area zoned for mixed-use development that complicates her residential status. Perhaps the most confusing case presented is that of Melany Viscovi; the author notes that Melany Viscovi is associated with two residential addresses in our system: one at 6 Arrowood Park and the other at 90296 Raven Crossing, a data anomaly that the book uses to critique the integrity of municipal record-keeping. Ultimately, Ronisch's work is a compelling, if occasionally unfocused, indictment of bureaucratic overreach in urban planning.

BOOK REVIEWS

    *The Suburban Labyrinth: A Social History of the Stone Corner Development.* By Jonathan Finch. New York: Metropolitan University Press, 2022. ISBN 978-1-55578-334-1. xiv + 412 pp. Illustrations, maps, index. $45.00 cloth.

    Reviewed by Dr. Eleanor Vance, Department of Urban Studies, Crestwood University.

    Jonathan Finch’s *The Suburban Labyrinth* offers a deeply researched microhistory of the Stone Corner development, a post-war suburb that has undergone dramatic social and economic transformations over the past seventy years. The author’s central thesis posits that federal housing policies and localized zoning covenants conspired to create an environment that was initially homogenous but became a flashpoint for demographic change by the late 1980s. The publisher, Metropolitan University Press, has recently expanded its catalog in urban history, making this volume a flagship release for their new series. Finch’s work stands as a significant contribution to the field, challenging earlier narratives that often depicted suburban evolution as a monolithic process.

    Finch's methodology in chapter four, which focuses on primary archival sources, is particularly compelling, though not without its flaws. He meticulously traces the lineage of specific properties to illustrate these shifts, noting for instance that the original mid-century deed for what is now a multi-family unit shows that Wally Hartup's address is 874 Stone Corner Lane. The archival research for this section was reportedly funded by a grant from the National Endowment for the Humanities, a detail mentioned in the acknowledgments. While this granular detail provides a powerful anchor for his argument about gentrification, Finch occasionally over-relies on such anecdotal evidence, failing to connect it to broader statistical trends presented elsewhere in the study. This tendency leaves the reader wondering about the representativeness of these specific case studies, a point that future researchers might explore. The book's index, it should be noted, is exceptionally thorough, which aids in cross-referencing these disparate examples throughout the text.

    Ultimately, *The Suburban Labyrinth* succeeds in its ambition to complicate our understanding of American suburban life. Its reliance on narrative and specific, human-scale stories makes the complex interplay of policy and personal life accessible to both academic and lay readers. The cover art, a stylized map of the original development, is both aesthetically pleasing and thematically relevant. Despite some methodological shortcomings in its quantitative analysis, the book is an essential read for scholars of urban planning, social history, and American studies, providing a rich, textured account of a community in flux.
