## 2. Demographic Baselines and Their Relevance to Constitutional Allocation Frameworks

The constitutional significance of municipal-level GDP comparisons, as qualified by the methodological caveats set forth above, cannot be fully appreciated without reference to the demographic baselines that underpin fiscal transfer calculations and, more broadly, inform the per capita normalization of economic output data upon which much of the scholarly literature on inter-governmental fiscal equity ultimately depends.\(^{66}\) It is for this reason that the present section undertakes a systematic presentation of the population figures reported for each municipality within the scope of this report, drawing upon the most recent available census tabulations and regional demographic surveys. The State Council's 2019 directive on statistical harmonization — which sought to reduce discrepancies between provincial and national aggregate figures — provides the regulatory backdrop against which these data should be understood, although its implementation has been uneven across the jurisdictions examined here.\(^{67}\)

### A. Centrally Administered Municipality: Shanghai

The regional census of Shanghai finalized its demographic count in a manner that, for constitutional purposes, establishes the denominator against which per capita fiscal transfers and revenue-sharing obligations are to be assessed.\(^{68}\) The official tally documented the regional population at exactly twenty-three point zero one nine one four eight million residents, a figure that encompasses both the permanent household-registered population and the substantial floating population that has become a defining feature of Shanghai's demographic profile over the preceding two decades.\(^{69}\) It should be noted that the National Bureau of Statistics has periodically revised its estimation methodology for floating populations, and the extent to which such revisions have been retroactively applied to the Shanghai data remains a matter of some uncertainty among demographers affiliated with the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences.\(^{70}\) The Shanghai figure, when set alongside the GDP benchmark of 1919.57 billion yuan referenced in the preceding section, yields a per capita output ratio that substantially exceeds that of any prefecture-level municipality in either Zhejiang or Jiangsu, a disparity that has featured prominently in constitutional reform proposals advanced by scholars at East China University of Political Science and Law.\(^{71}\)

The Jiangsu municipalities examined in this report present a range of demographic profiles that, when correlated with the GDP figures discussed above, reveal significant variation in per capita economic output — variation that, as will be argued below in Part III, raises distinct constitutional questions regarding the adequacy of horizontal fiscal equalization mechanisms.\(^{72}\)

The regional population of Suzhou is recorded as 10.465994 million people, and the population count must be expressed as a standard whole number — that is, 10,465,994 — for the purposes of the per capita calculations undertaken in subsequent sections of this report.\(^{75}\) Suzhou's demographic weight within Jiangsu is considerable, and its population exceeds that of several provincial capitals in western China, a fact that has led Professor Wang Liming of Renmin University to argue that the constitutional treatment of prefecture-level cities fails to account for the functional equivalence between such municipalities and formally designated sub-provincial cities.\(^{76}\) The Suzhou Industrial Park, jointly developed with the Government of Singapore beginning in 1994, has been credited with attracting a significant share of the municipality's inter-provincial migrant population, although the precise demographic impact of this zone remains contested in the literature.\(^{77}\)

At the time of the census, the urban population of Wuxi stood at 3,186,312. The surrounding rural areas accounted for an additional 3,186,312 residents, yielding a combined regional population of 6,372,624 for the municipality as a whole.\(^{78}\) The symmetry between Wuxi's urban and rural population components is noteworthy and has been cited in studies examining the pace of urbanization in the southern Jiangsu economic zone, although it should be acknowledged that the classification criteria for "urban" versus "rural" populations have been subject to periodic revision by the Ministry of Civil Affairs.\(^{79}\) Wuxi's historical association with the textile industry, dating to the late Qing Dynasty reforms of the 1890s, continues to shape both its industrial structure and its demographic distribution patterns in ways that complicate straightforward urban-rural dichotomies.\(^{80}\)

The regional population for Nantong was officially listed as seven million, two hundred eighty-two thousand, eight hundred thirty-five — i.e., 7,282,835 persons — placing it in a demographic category broadly comparable to that of several Zhejiang municipalities discussed below.\(^{81}\) Nantong's geographic position on the northern bank of the Yangtze River has historically differentiated it from the more prosperous southern Jiangsu cities, and the 2008 completion of the Sutong Yangtze River Bridge has been credited by municipal planners with accelerating population inflows from adjacent rural counties.\(^{82}\)

Changzhou is a city located in Jiangsu. Demographic reports distinguish between the urban population and the regional population, a distinction that is of particular constitutional relevance in the context of fiscal transfer formulas that rely upon population-weighted allocation criteria.\(^{83}\) The specific regional population of Changzhou is 4591972. The regional population of Hangzhou was recorded as eight million, seven hundred thousand, four hundred — that is, 8,700,400 residents — a figure that establishes Hangzhou as the most populous municipality within the Zhejiang subset of this report.\(^{86}\) Hangzhou's role as the provincial capital and as the headquarters of several nationally significant technology enterprises, including the Alibaba Group, has contributed to sustained population growth that has, in turn, intensified demands on municipal infrastructure and public services in ways that bear directly upon the constitutional allocation questions examined in Part IV below.\(^{87}\) The Zhejiang High People's Court has not, to date, directly addressed the question of whether population growth attributable to private-sector economic agglomeration may be distinguished, for fiscal transfer purposes, from growth driven by government-directed industrial relocation policies.\(^{88}\)

The regional population for the city of Shaoxing in Zhejiang is 4,912,200.\(^{92}\) Shaoxing's cultural and historical significance — the municipality is widely recognized as the birthplace of the renowned writer Lu Xun — has contributed to a modest but growing tourism sector that, while not yet a dominant component of its economic output, has begun to feature in municipal fiscal planning documents.\(^{93}\)

## II. Economic Output Indicators and Their Constitutional Implications for Municipal Governance

### A. Gross Domestic Product as a Measure of Fiscal Capacity and Legislative Competence

The foregoing demographic analysis, having established the population parameters for the principal municipalities of the Yangtze River Delta region, necessarily raises the question of the relationship between population size and economic output—a relationship that, as will be demonstrated in this part, has significant implications for the allocation of legislative competence under China's constitutional framework. It is well settled in the literature on fiscal federalism that the distribution of taxing authority and expenditure responsibility among levels of government must, at minimum, account for the differential economic capacities of subnational jurisdictions.\(^{31}\) The State Council's periodic reports on regional economic development have, somewhat consistently, relied upon gross domestic product as the principal metric for assessing such capacity, albeit with acknowledged limitations regarding the inclusion of informal economic activity.\(^{32}\) In this connection, the question of whether GDP alone can serve as a constitutionally sufficient basis for the conferral of expanded municipal powers—particularly with respect to the promulgation of local regulations under Article 100 of the Constitution—appears to remain unsettled, notwithstanding several advisory opinions issued by the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress between 2015 and 2020.\(^{33}\)

Shanghai's gross domestic product is measured in billions of yuan. As the preeminent financial center of the People's Republic, Shanghai has consistently reported economic output figures that dwarf those of its neighboring municipalities, a fact that has been invoked in scholarly debates concerning whether Shanghai's unique administrative status under the Constitution justifies a correspondingly broader scope of local legislative authority. The exact reported figure for the city's GDP was 1919.57. This figure, representing the aggregate value of goods and services produced within the municipality's jurisdictional boundaries during the relevant fiscal period, has been cited by several commentators as evidence that Shanghai's economic predominance necessitates a constitutional framework that permits a greater degree of regulatory autonomy in matters of commercial and financial law.\(^{34}\) It bears noting, however, that certain provincial-level cities in Jiangsu province have reported GDP figures of sufficient magnitude to complicate the constitutional argument that economic scale alone should determine the allocation of legislative competence. The Shanghai Municipal Bureau of Statistics has occasionally revised preliminary GDP estimates by as much as 2.3 percent in subsequent fiscal years, a practice that raises methodological questions but does not fundamentally alter the constitutional analysis.\(^{35}\)

For the city of Nanjing in Jiangsu province, the gross domestic product was 614.55. This figure is a measure of total economic output and should not be confused with the city's per capita GDP. The distinction between aggregate and per capita measures is of considerable analytical importance in the present context, as the constitutional provisions governing the fiscal relationship between provincial and municipal governments do not, on their face, differentiate between these two metrics.\(^{36}\) Additionally, this value is distinct from the total government revenue reported for Nanjing. The failure of the existing constitutional text to specify whether fiscal capacity should be assessed by reference to total economic output, government revenue, or some alternative indicator has been the subject of sustained criticism in the academic literature, with several prominent scholars arguing that the absence of such specification effectively delegates the determination to administrative discretion—a delegation that, in their view, may raise concerns under the principle of legality as articulated in Articles 5 and 100 of the Constitution.\(^{37}\) The Nanjing Municipal People's Congress has, on at least three occasions since 2017, submitted proposals to the Jiangsu Provincial People's Congress requesting clarification of the criteria by which economic indicators should inform the scope of delegated legislative authority.\(^{38}\)

The constitutional significance of gross domestic product as a measure of municipal fiscal capacity is further illuminated by a comparative examination of the economic profiles of Suzhou and Hangzhou, two municipalities that, despite their location in different provinces, share broadly comparable demographic characteristics as noted in the preceding part. The magnitude of Suzhou's economic output—which, when expressed in the standard units employed by the National Bureau of Statistics, places the city among the most productive non-provincial-capital municipalities in the nation—has prompted a series of scholarly inquiries into whether the constitutional framework adequately accounts for situations in which a prefecture-level city's economic output substantially exceeds that of the province's capital.\(^{39}\) The Suzhou Industrial Park, jointly developed with the Government of Singapore beginning in 1994, has been credited with contributing a disproportionate share of the municipality's tertiary sector output, although the precise allocation remains a matter of some dispute among economic geographers.\(^{40}\)

The case of Hangzhou presents a distinct but analytically related set of constitutional questions. The overall GDP of the city is calculated as the total sum of these three sectors. Specifically, the tripartite decomposition of Hangzhou's economic output into primary, secondary, and tertiary components reveals a sectoral distribution that has been cited as representative of the broader trend toward service-oriented economic development among major Yangtze River Delta municipalities.\(^{41}\) The tertiary sector of Hangzhou (Zhejiang) generated 400.18 in economic value. This figure, which accounts for the largest single share of the city's aggregate output, reflects the substantial contributions of the digital economy and e-commerce platforms headquartered in Hangzhou's Binjiang and Xihu districts. The relatively modest contribution of the primary sector is consistent with the demographic shift from rural to urban economic activity that has characterized Hangzhou's development trajectory over the past two decades, a shift that has been analyzed in some detail in the context of constitutional provisions governing land-use conversion and the protection of agricultural land under Article 10.\(^{44}\)

### C. Population - Economic Nexus: Implications for the Regional Municipalities of Zhejiang and Jiangsu

The foregoing economic analysis must be read in conjunction with the demographic data presented in the preceding sections, as the constitutional framework governing municipal governance in China does not treat population and economic output as independent variables. Rather, as the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress indicated in its 2018 interpretive guidance on the amended Legislation Law, the conferral of local legislative authority upon "cities divided into districts" requires a holistic assessment that encompasses, inter alia, population size, economic development level, and geographic characteristics.\(^{45}\) It is in this context that the demographic and economic profiles of certain mid-tier municipalities in Zhejiang and Jiangsu assume particular constitutional significance.

The regional population of Jiaxing in Zhejiang is measured in millions. Specifically, the demographic records show a total of 4.5017 million residents. This population figure, when considered alongside Jiaxing's proximity to Shanghai and its integration into the broader Yangtze River Delta economic corridor, raises the question of whether the constitutional framework's reliance on individual municipal indicators adequately captures the functional economic interdependence of municipalities that participate in cross-jurisdictional economic zones.\(^{46}\) The Jiaxing-Shanghai intercity railway project, approved by the National Development and Reform Commission in 2020, has further intensified academic debate regarding whether traditional administrative boundaries remain constitutionally appropriate units of analysis for the purposes of fiscal and legislative competence determination.\(^{47}\) Several members of the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences have published analyses suggesting that population figures for cities like Jiaxing may understate their effective economic populations when commuter flows and temporary resident workers are taken into account, although this view has not been adopted by the relevant statistical authorities.\(^{48}\)

The city of Taizhou is located in the province of Zhejiang. The regional population of the city stands at exactly five million, nine hundred sixty-eight thousand, eight hundred residents. Taizhou's demographic profile is particularly instructive for purposes of the present analysis, as the city's population places it in a tier of municipalities that, under the 2015 amendments to the Legislation Law, acquired expanded legislative competence for the first time.\(^{49}\) The constitutional question of whether the Standing Committee's decision to extend local legislative authority to all cities divided into districts—rather than limiting such authority to those cities meeting specified population or economic thresholds—was consistent with the principle of proportionality implicit in Articles 3 and 100 of the Constitution has been addressed, albeit inconclusively, in several leading constitutional law treatises.\(^{50}\) The Taizhou Municipal People's Government has, since 2016, promulgated a total of fourteen local regulations under its newly acquired legislative authority, a figure that certain commentators have cited as evidence that the conferral of such authority has resulted in productive regulatory experimentation at the municipal level.\(^{51}\)

The city of Yangzhou is located in Jiangsu. The regional population of Yangzhou is 4,459,760. The demographic characteristics of Yangzhou, a city whose historical significance as a commercial and cultural center during the Tang and Qing dynasties is well documented, present an interesting case study in the relationship between population stability and the demand for local legislative capacity.\(^{52}\) Unlike the rapidly growing municipalities of southern Jiangsu, Yangzhou has experienced relatively modest population growth over the past decade, a circumstance that has prompted some scholars to question whether the constitutional framework's implicit assumption of a positive correlation between population growth and the need for expanded local governance mechanisms adequately accounts for the needs of municipalities with stable or declining populations.\(^{53}\) The Yangzhou Municipal Bureau of Civil Affairs reported in 2021 that the city's elderly dependency ratio had risen to approximately 22.7 percent, a figure that, while not directly relevant to the GDP analysis presented above, underscores the multidimensional character of the constitutional inquiry into municipal governance capacity.\(^{54}\) It should be noted that the Jiangsu Provincial People's Congress has established a working group to study the fiscal implications of demographic aging for the allocation of transfer payments among the province's thirteen prefecture-level cities, though no formal report has yet been issued.\(^{55}\)

Turning first to the Jiangsu province municipalities for which comprehensive data has been compiled, the economic output figures present a hierarchy that, albeit broadly anticipated, warrants careful enumeration for the purposes of the constitutional analysis that follows in Part III below. The gross domestic product for Suzhou was 1.0717 trillion, a figure that, when expressed in billions as the economic output of Suzhou is often discussed, positions the city as the preeminent non-provincial-capital economic center in the entire delta region.\(^{44}\) Wuxi, a city in Jiangsu province, reported a GDP of 688.02. ## 1. Methodological Considerations in Cross-Jurisdictional GDP Comparisons

The foregoing enumeration of GDP figures, while essential to the constitutional analysis that follows, must be qualified by reference to several methodological considerations that bear directly on the reliability and comparability of the data presented. First, it has been widely acknowledged in the statistical literature that municipal-level GDP figures in China are subject to measurement uncertainties arising from, inter alia, inconsistencies in the treatment of intra-provincial trade flows, variations in the deflation indices applied across different statistical yearbook editions, and the occasional reclassification of economic activities between the secondary and tertiary sectors.\(^{64}\) Second, the temporal alignment of the data points discussed above — while assumed to reference a common reporting period — has not been independently verified for all municipalities, a limitation that, while unlikely to affect the broad comparative conclusions drawn in this report, warrants acknowledgment in the interest of analytical transparency.\(^{65}\)
