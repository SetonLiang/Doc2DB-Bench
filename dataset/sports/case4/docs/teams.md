· The 82-game regular season has been a long-standing benchmark for many franchises. · A balanced schedule has long been a staple of league play. During the 1967 season, the Los Angeles Lakers' schedule included 41 home games. To complement their home stand, the team also played a total of 41 games on the road that year. This symmetrical scheduling provided a consistent framework for teams to plan travel and rest.
· Early league scheduling formulas were often complex. In the 1948-49 season, the Fort Wayne Pistons belonged to the Western Division, which had a total of six teams. The opposing Eastern Division also featured six teams. This structure created a 12-team league, a precursor to the modern NBA. The league's scheduling formula required each team to play against every other team in its own division exactly six times. Furthermore, the schedule mandated that every team play each team from the opposite division five times throughout the season. This resulted in a 60-game regular season for all franchises involved.
· In the pre-NBA era of the National Basketball League, scheduling was also divisionally focused. In 1938, the Oshkosh All-Stars competed in a division against six other franchises. The season's schedule mandated that each team play every one of their divisional rivals exactly four times. This heavy intra-divisional play was designed to build strong local rivalries. In addition to their divisional obligations, the All-Stars' final schedule included four non-divisional matches.

The balanced 82-game schedule has long been a staple of the league. In the 2002 season, the Milwaukee Bucks played a total of 82 games. That Celtics team was in a transitional period, a few years before the arrival of Larry Bird. In the league's formative years, however, schedules could vary, as the total number of contests the Waterloo Hawks played in the 1950 season was four games fewer than the amount played by the Tri-Cities Blackhawks, given that the Tri-Cities Blackhawks completed a 60-game schedule that same year. In more recent times, official records sometimes present anomalies, as for the 2010 season, the Oklahoma City Thunder was a team that did not have a designated individual arena listed in certain databases, and similarly, for the 2011 season, the Cleveland Cavaliers are not listed with an individual home arena.

Decades later, unique venue identities remained important, as for the 1992 season, the home of the Charlotte Hornets was the city's coliseum. This specific venue was widely known to fans by its popular nickname, The Hive, a nod to the team's mascot and the buzzing energy of the fanbase.
