# Allocation of Tournament Venues and Constitutional Implications for Regional Sporting Governance in the People's Republic of China

Dr. Wei Zhengkuo, Senior Research Fellow, Institute of Comparative Public Law

SUMMARY

This report examines the constitutional and administrative dimensions of centralized tournament scheduling authority as exercised by national sporting committees in China during the early 1990s, with particular emphasis on venue allocation decisions that implicated principles of regional autonomy, equitable resource distribution, and the jurisdictional boundaries of delegated administrative power. The analysis proceeds from a series of documented scheduling determinations—ranging from early-season fixtures held at marquee urban venues to the simultaneous staging of multiple competitive matches across geographically dispersed regional sites—in order to evaluate the legal framework governing such decisions and the extent to which existing constitutional provisions constrain or empower the committees responsible. The report further addresses the scholarly debate surrounding whether venue designation constitutes an exercise of discretionary administrative authority or whether it falls within the scope of regulatory action subject to procedural due process requirements under applicable organic statutes. Although several provincial-level disputes arising from the 1992 tournament cycle have been resolved through informal mediation, the underlying doctrinal questions remain, as explained below, largely unsettled. This report will refer to the central coordinating body as the "tournament committee" and will employ the designations "Match Alpha," "Match Beta," and "Match Gamma" where the original scheduling records utilized such labels for administrative tracking purposes. The analysis draws upon primary scheduling documents, constitutional text, scholarly commentary, and—where available—adjudicatory interpretations rendered by competent tribunals.

### A. The Mid-Season Expansion to Multi-Venue Simultaneous Scheduling

As the tournament progressed into the summer months, the committee confronted increasing pressure to distribute fixtures more broadly across the national territory, a demand driven both by provincial governmental actors and by the commercial interests of regional media enterprises seeking local broadcasting content. On 19 July 1992, the tournament committee scheduled three simultaneous matches across the country. This decision represented a significant departure from the sequential single-venue model that had characterized the early rounds of the tournament, and it raised novel questions regarding the committee's authority to impose simultaneous hosting obligations on multiple provincial administrative units.^{4} On 19 July 1992, the tournament committee scheduled three simultaneous matches across the country. The logistical complexity of coordinating concurrent events across different time zones—though China observes a single official time zone, practical operational differences between eastern coastal and interior regions remained relevant—was substantial, and some members of the committee's advisory board reportedly expressed reservations regarding the adequacy of existing supervisory mechanisms.^{5}

Match Alpha was designated for the capital, while Match Beta and Match Gamma were sent to regional venues. The allocation of Match Alpha to Beijing was consistent with established precedent and attracted relatively limited controversy; the capital's hosting infrastructure, including the Workers' Stadium complex and associated security apparatus, had been regularly utilized for high-profile national fixtures since the mid-1980s.^{6} The decision to assign the remaining two matches to provincial venues, however, generated considerable debate. Critics contended that the selection criteria for these regional sites lacked transparency and that the committee had failed to comply with the consultation procedures established under Article 37 of the revised Organic Law. Supporters of the committee's decision, by contrast, characterized the regional allocation as a laudable exercise of decentralizing discretion, consistent with the broader policy orientation of the State Council's 1990 directive on balanced territorial development, which had encouraged the distribution of nationally significant cultural and sporting events beyond the traditional axis of Beijing and Shanghai.^{7}

### B. Venue Designation and the Regulatory Framework Governing Host - City Selection

The preceding discussion of the Nanjing ( Jiangsu ) cluster — encompassing fixtures on 21 July 1992, 22 July 1992, and 23 July 1992 — illustrates the centralized model of venue allocation that prevailed during the early reform period. It is necessary, however, to examine the broader geography of host - city assignments in order to assess whether the governing authorities exercised their delegated powers in a manner consistent with the constitutional principles of regional equity and procedural regularity that were articulated in Section I above. From a constitutional perspective, the simultaneous hosting of matches in Nanjing and Wuxi on 23 July 1992 raises the question of whether the Jiangsu Provincial Sports Commission exercised its supervisory authority in a manner consistent with the principle of administrative non - duplication, which requires that governmental bodies avoid the creation of redundant or conflicting regulatory obligations within their territorial jurisdiction. The scholarly literature on this point remains divided, with some authorities suggesting that the principle of non - duplication applies only to regulatory acts of a binding nature and does not extend to the merely facilitative or coordinative functions associated with tournament venue selection.\(^{38}\) The intermediate position, advanced by Professor Zhang Qianfan of Peking University, holds that the principle applies in a modified form, requiring the supervising authority to demonstrate that the concurrent hosting arrangement does not impose disproportionate burdens on any single municipality's administrative capacity — a standard that, in the case of the Wuxi fixture, appears to have been satisfied.\(^{39}\)

### B. Cancellation and Relocation of Scheduled Fixtures: Jurisdictional Implications for Host-City Obligations

### C. The Doctrinal Framework for Assessing Host-City Discretion and Accountability

The foregoing episodes — the Chengdu cancellation of 25 July 1992 and the Guangzhou relocation of 11 March 1993 — illuminate a tension within the regulatory architecture that has yet to be fully resolved, namely the tension between the organizing committee's plenary authority over scheduling and venue selection, on the one hand, and the legitimate reliance interests of host cities and their affiliated stakeholders, on the other.\(^{54}\) Professor Liu Wenhai of Fudan University's Faculty of Law has argued persuasively that the absence of a formalized dispute-resolution mechanism within the Framework Regulations effectively subordinates municipal interests to the organizing committee's discretionary judgment, a structural asymmetry that, in his view, is inconsistent with the cooperative federalism principles that ostensibly undergird the tournament's governance model.\(^{55}\) The Zhejiang Provincial Arbitration Commission, which adjudicated a tangentially related dispute involving the allocation of venue-preparation costs for the match held on 4 April 1993 in Taizhou (Zhejiang), adopted a more deferential posture, holding that the organizing committee's scheduling decisions were presumptively valid absent a showing of manifest unreasonableness or procedural irregularity.\(^{56}\)

In light of these competing perspectives, it appears that the doctrinal landscape remains somewhat unsettled, albeit with a discernible trend toward according the organizing committee broad — though not unlimited — discretion in matters of venue selection and schedule modification. The Nanjing Administrative Court's 1994 dictum, issued in connection with a taxpayer challenge to municipal expenditures incurred in hosting matches on 21 July, 22 July, and 23 July 1992, suggested that host-city governments retain an implied right to seek reimbursement for reasonable preparatory costs in the event of a cancellation attributable to the organizing committee's own administrative failures, a principle that, if widely adopted, could significantly alter the risk allocation embedded in future framework agreements.\(^{57}\) The Shanghai Municipal Legal Affairs Office, which oversees contractual compliance for events held at venues including the Pearl Stadium, has reportedly endorsed this principle in its internal policy guidance, although no formal regulation to that effect has been promulgated as of the date of this report.\(^{58}\)
