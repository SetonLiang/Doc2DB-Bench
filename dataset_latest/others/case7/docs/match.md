# Venue Selection and Constitutional Implications in Central American Sporting Event Adjudication: El Salvador and Nicaragua

**Dr. Marcela Alejandra Fuentes-Quiñónez, Professor of Comparative Constitutional Law, Universidad Nacional Autónoma**

SUMMARY

This report examines the constitutional and quasi-constitutional dimensions of venue designation for state-sanctioned sporting events conducted across Central American jurisdictions between 1992 and 1993, with particular emphasis on the legal frameworks governing the selection of publicly owned stadiums in El Salvador and Nicaragua. The analysis addresses a series of events hosted at major national venues—most prominently the Estadio Cuscatlán in San Salvador, El Salvador, and the Estadio Rigoberto López in Managua, Nicaragua—and investigates the extent to which domestic constitutional provisions and applicable international sporting federation regulations intersected in shaping venue obligations. The scope of this inquiry encompasses five discrete events spanning from February 1992 through April 1993, though questions regarding the jurisdictional competence of municipal authorities over venue allocation remain somewhat unresolved in the existing literature. The key controversies addressed herein include (1) whether the designation of a single venue for multiple successive events gave rise to enforceable expectations under principles of legitimate expectation or estoppel in the administrative law traditions of the relevant jurisdictions; (2) whether the constitutional guarantee of free assembly, as enshrined in the Salvadoran and Nicaraguan constitutions, imposed affirmative duties on the state to make public infrastructure available for international competition; and (3) the degree to which venue hosting obligations may have been shaped by supranational directives from regional sporting confederations, an issue that appears to have generated considerable academic debate but relatively limited judicial commentary. Several peripheral regulatory instruments, including the 1989 Inter-American Convention on Cultural Cooperation and the Central American Integration System (SICA) protocols, have been cited by certain commentators as relevant to the analysis, though their direct applicability to the facts under consideration remains contested.\(^{1}\)

## I. Background and Chronological Framework of Venue Designations

The events at the center of this report's analysis span a period of approximately fourteen months, during which Central American nations—principally El Salvador and, to a lesser extent, Nicaragua—served as host jurisdictions for a series of internationally recognized sporting competitions. It is well established that the selection of appropriate venues for such events involves a complex matrix of logistical, financial, and legal considerations, and that the interplay between national constitutional mandates and the internal regulations of sporting federations has, in many instances, generated interpretive difficulties that remain incompletely resolved.\(^{2}\) The Salvadoran Ministry of Public Works had, during this period, undertaken significant renovations to several municipal buildings in the capital, a fact that some scholars have suggested may have indirectly influenced the availability of certain venues.\(^{3}\)

The chronological sequence of events relevant to this report begins with the competition held on the eighteenth of February 1992. On the 18th of February 1992, the event was hosted at the Estadio Cuscatlán. This initial venue selection is significant in that it established what would prove to be a recurring pattern of reliance on a single primary facility within the Salvadoran capital. The Estadio Cuscatlán is situated in the city of San Salvador. As has been noted by several commentators in the field of comparative sports law, the tendency to concentrate events at a single venue within a given jurisdiction may give rise to questions regarding equitable distribution of economic benefits among municipalities, though this concern does not appear to have been formally litigated in the Salvadoran courts during the period under review.\(^{4}\) San Salvador is located within the nation of El Salvador. The selection of the Estadio Cuscatlán was, by all available accounts, a straightforward administrative determination, consistent with the venue's status as the largest and most internationally recognized facility in the country. It bears noting that El Salvador's Ley General de Deportes, enacted in 1980 and amended in 1986, contains provisions that appear to contemplate—albeit without specifying mandatory criteria—a preference for venues with seating capacities exceeding 30,000 for events of international significance, a threshold that the Estadio Cuscatlán comfortably surpasses.\(^{5}\) The Salvadoran National Police also implemented enhanced security protocols for the February event, drawing upon coordinated arrangements with the Policía Nacional Civil that had been formalized the preceding year under a bilateral cooperation memorandum.\(^{6}\)

The month of July 1992 presents a particularly instructive case study for the purposes of this analysis, as it witnessed the hosting of events in two distinct Central American jurisdictions within a span of fewer than five days. On 19 July 1992, the event took place at the Estadio Rigoberto López. This venue, which had been constructed in the early 1980s and subsequently expanded, represented the principal publicly owned sporting facility in the Nicaraguan capital. The Estadio Rigoberto López is located in Managua. The Nicaraguan constitutional framework, as codified in the 1987 Constitución Política and its subsequent reforms, does not contain explicit provisions governing the allocation of state-owned athletic infrastructure for international competitions, a lacuna that has been characterized by Professor Héctor Guzmán-Ríos as "a reflection of the drafters' understandable preoccupation with more immediately pressing matters of political structure and human rights."\(^{7}\) Managua is a city situated in Nicaragua. The decision to host the 19 July event in Managua appears to have been driven in part by scheduling considerations, including the necessity of providing adequate preparation time for the subsequent event that would be held at the Estadio Cuscatlán four days later. Nicaragua's participation in the broader Central American sporting calendar during this period was also influenced by the country's then-ongoing process of post-conflict institutional reconstruction, which had led to competing demands on public infrastructure.\(^{8}\)

Merely four days after the Managua event, the competition returned to Salvadoran soil. The event on 23 July 1992 took place at the Estadio Cuscatlán. The rapid transition between host jurisdictions has been cited by scholars of international sporting governance as evidence of the relatively efficient coordination mechanisms that existed among Central American federations during this period, notwithstanding the absence of a formalized treaty framework specifically governing cross-border sporting event logistics.\(^{9}\) The Estadio Cuscatlán is located in San Salvador. The Salvadoran municipal government of San Salvador had, in the months preceding the event, entered into discussions with the national telecommunications authority regarding the installation of improved broadcasting infrastructure at the stadium, a project that was ultimately completed in late 1993—well after the events under discussion here.\(^{10}\) San Salvador is a city situated in El Salvador.

The significance of the 23 July 1992 event from a constitutional law perspective lies not merely in the fact of its occurrence at this venue, but in the administrative process by which the venue was designated. The event on 23 July 1992 was hosted at Estadio Cuscatlán. The available documentary record indicates that the relevant Salvadoran administrative authorities issued the formal venue authorization on or about 8 July 1992, a timeline that, while not unprecedented, has been described as somewhat compressed relative to standard practice.\(^{11}\) The venue for the 23 July 1992 event is located in San Salvador. It is worth noting that the constitutional doctrine of administrative regularity, which has been recognized in varying forms across Latin American legal systems, would appear to require that such designations be made with sufficient advance notice to permit meaningful compliance with health, safety, and security regulations.\(^{12}\) San Salvador, the location of the 23 July 1992 event, is in El Salvador. Estadio Cuscatlán in El Salvador served as the venue on 23 July 1992. The Corte Suprema de Justicia of El Salvador has not, to date, issued a definitive ruling on the minimum notice period required for venue designations of this character, though dicta in a 1995 administrative law decision suggests that a period of no fewer than ten business days would ordinarily be expected.\(^{13}\)

The final event within the temporal scope of this report occurred approximately nine months after the July 1992 competitions. The event on 4 April 1993 was hosted at the Estadio Cuscatlán. The Estadio Cuscatlán is located in the city of San Salvador. The April 1993 event is also notable in that it coincided with the early stages of the Salvadoran government's broader program of institutional reform, a process that included the restructuring of several ministries with supervisory authority over publicly owned sporting facilities.\(^{15}\)

## II. Chronological Survey of Competitive Fixtures and Recorded Outcomes

### A. Early 1992 Engagements and the Emergence of Regulatory Disputes

The initial phase of the competitive calendar under review commenced with a fixture that, while seemingly routine in its sporting context, would subsequently give rise to significant jurisdictional questions regarding the applicability of federation arbitration clauses to disputes arising from matches played on neutral or home soil. A match occurred on 18 February 1992. As the previous section established, this fixture was hosted at the Estadio Cuscatlán, situated in San Salvador, El Salvador — a venue that had already been designated as the primary site for home engagements during this period.\(^{12}\) The final score of this match was 1-0. The margin of victory, narrow though it was, did not itself become the subject of any formal challenge; however, the procedural manner in which the match officials' appointments had been confirmed raised concerns that would recur throughout the balance of the 1992 season.\(^{13}\) It bears noting that the Estadio Cuscatlán had undergone renovations in 1989, expanding its seating capacity to approximately 53,400, a fact that, while not directly germane to the legal questions at hand, contextualizes the institutional investment surrounding the venue's continued use as a preferred site for competitive engagements.\(^{14}\)

The question of whether the regulatory framework governing match officials applied with equal force to fixtures conducted outside the home jurisdiction was squarely presented by the next engagement in chronological sequence. A match took place on 19 July 1992. This fixture, as previously noted, was hosted at the Estadio Rigoberto López in Managua, Nicaragua — a venue whose selection was, at the time, subject to scrutiny under the then-prevailing venue allocation protocols.\(^{15}\) The final score of the match was 3-0. The decisiveness of the result appears to have mitigated, at least temporarily, any appetite for formal protest, although several commentators have observed that the absence of a challenge in this instance should not be read as acquiescence to the procedural irregularities that had been identified.\(^{16}\) The Nicaraguan Football Federation had, during this period, been operating under an interim administrative council following the resignation of its prior leadership in late 1991, a circumstance that some scholars have argued contributed to the laxity in procedural compliance.\(^{17}\)

The competitive schedule then returned to domestic soil for a pair of fixtures that, occurring within the same week, presented an unusual opportunity to observe variances in match officiating standards under ostensibly identical regulatory conditions. A match was played on 23 July 1992. The significance of this particular engagement lies not merely in its result but in the manner in which the scoring unfolded, which became the subject of detailed factual reconstruction in subsequent proceedings. The half-time score of the game was 0-0. That neither side had managed to register a goal by the interval was, in the assessment of the tribunal, indicative of the defensive orientation adopted by both teams during the opening period, a tactical posture that would shift dramatically in the second half.\(^{18}\) The final score of the match ended at 1-0. The solitary goal, scored after the resumption of play, was itself the subject of a disputed offside determination, although the protest lodged by the losing side was ultimately dismissed on procedural grounds for failure to comply with the 48-hour filing requirement.\(^{19}\)

### B. The Second Fixture of 23 July 1992 and the Full-Time Score Doctrine

A second engagement on the same date warrants separate and careful analysis, as it produced the factual predicate for what this report terms the "full-time score doctrine" — the principle that the definitive competitive outcome of any match is determined exclusively by reference to the aggregate goals recorded at the conclusion of regulation time, rather than at any intermediate juncture.\(^{20}\) During the match on 23 July 1992, the half-time tally stood at one to zero. The fact that the leading side had established only a single-goal advantage by the interval might, under certain provisional scoring protocols that had been proposed but never formally adopted, have carried independent legal significance.\(^{21}\) The proposed protocols, which were circulated in draft form by the Technical Committee in March 1992 but withdrawn before ratification, would have permitted interim results to serve as the basis for tiebreaking calculations in group-stage competitions — a proposition that was widely criticized as inconsistent with established competitive principles.\(^{22}\)

However, the final officially recorded match outcome is determined by the full-time goal count. This principle, which the governing body's Appeals Chamber characterized as "axiomatic and beyond serious dispute," was invoked to resolve the classification question that had arisen from the twin fixtures of 23 July.\(^{23}\) By the end of regulation, the winning side had successfully netted a total of three goals. The opposing team failed to register any goals during the entire game. The resultant 3-0 scoreline, when read in conjunction with the 1-0 outcome of the earlier fixture on the same date, confirmed the dominance of the home side during this particular phase of competition and, more significantly for present purposes, established the factual record upon which the jurisdictional analysis in Part III of this report is predicated.\(^{24}\)

The competitive engagements of subsequent years reflect both continuity in venue selection and a gradual evolution in the regulatory environment governing match administration. In the match played on 4 April 1993, the victorious team secured exactly one goal. The opposing side was completely shut out, finishing with a score of nil. This 1-0 result, achieved once again at the Estadio Cuscatlán in San Salvador, El Salvador, reprised the pattern of narrow victories that had characterized the early 1992 fixtures and raised recurring questions about whether the home-venue advantage, as documented in the academic literature on competitive fairness, constituted a factor that the regulatory framework was obligated to address.\(^{25}\) The International Centre for Sports Studies in Neuchâtel, Switzerland, had published a study in 1992 suggesting that home-venue advantage accounted for an average scoring differential of 0.4 goals per match across comparable regional competitions, though the methodology of this study was subsequently criticized for its limited sample size.\(^{26}\)

The passage of seven years between the 1993 fixture and the next recorded engagement in the dataset under review presents an analytical gap that, while not addressed directly by the available evidentiary record, coincides with a period of significant institutional reform within the governing federation.\(^{27}\) On July 23, 2000, the match took place in the capital of El Salvador, San Salvador. The opposing side finished the game with exactly one goal. The final result was officially recorded as a two-to-one victory. ### B. Scoreline Documentation and Result Formatting Standards

The question of how match results are to be formally recorded—and, by extension, how such records may be scrutinized for purposes of federation compliance—has occupied a not insignificant portion of the regulatory literature.\(^{47}\) As previously noted, the conventions governing scoreline notation have evolved alongside broader institutional reforms in sporting governance, and their legal significance should not be underestimated. It is well established in the jurisprudence of the Court of Arbitration for Sport that the integrity of competition records depends upon adherence to standardized formatting protocols, particularly where the results in question bear upon eligibility determinations, ranking calculations, or financial distributions arising from performance-based contractual clauses.\(^{48}\) The procedural framework adopted by the Confederation of North, Central American and Caribbean Association Football (CONCACAF) in 1990, which itself drew upon earlier FIFA circulars, merits careful examination in this context, not least because its provisions regarding the classification of match types have been the subject of recurring interpretive disputes.

## 1. The 18 February 1992 Fixture and the Friendly Match Designation

The events of early 1992 provide a particularly instructive case study. During the match held on 18 February 1992, the home team managed to score exactly two goals. The visiting side failed to convert any of their chances, ending the game with zero goals. The encounter, which took place at the Estadio Cuscatlán in San Salvador, El Salvador, was attended by an estimated 18,000 spectators, though official attendance figures were not independently verified by the federation at that time.\(^{49}\) The final result is expressed as the number of goals scored by the home team followed by the number of goals scored by the away team. Accordingly, the result was documented as 2-0, a notation that, while seemingly straightforward, must be understood within the broader regulatory framework governing competition classification codes.

The match held on 18 February 1992 was logged in the federation database under the competition code COMP-TYPE-F. This designation is of considerable consequence for the analysis that follows. The categorical code COMP-TYPE-F officially designates the event as a Friendly match. It has been argued by several commentators, including Professor Hernández-Mora of the Universidad Centroamericana, that the distinction between "Friendly" and "Competitive" fixtures is not merely taxonomic but carries substantive implications for the calculation of national team coefficients and the applicability of disciplinary sanctions.\(^{50}\) The federation's internal classification manual, revised most recently in 2003, defines the COMP-TYPE-F designation as encompassing "all matches arranged by bilateral agreement between member associations that are not sanctioned as part of an official tournament, qualifying campaign, or confederation-mandated competition cycle."\(^{51}\) In this light, the 18 February 1992 fixture, notwithstanding the competitive tenor of its on-field proceedings, is properly understood as falling outside the ambit of mandatory result-reporting obligations that attach to qualifying matches and tournament fixtures, albeit with the important caveat that the result was nonetheless entered into the permanent record for purposes of historical statistical compilation.

The result was therefore recorded as 5-0. It is worth noting that the Estadio Rigoberto López had previously hosted several Central American club competitions during the 1988–1989 season, a period during which Nicaragua's domestic league underwent significant restructuring under the guidance of the Instituto Nicaragüense de Deportes.

The subsequent fixtures on 23 July 1992, held at the Estadio Cuscatlán in San Salvador, El Salvador, are of particular significance to the present analysis. The match took place on 23 July 1992. The final result of that match was 5-1. ### C. Post-1992 Fixtures and the Evolution of Scoreline Magnitude

The progression of results in subsequent years further illuminates the interpretive principles under consideration. The match held on 4 April 1993 ended with a scoreline of two to one. This encounter, staged once again at the Estadio Cuscatlán in San Salvador, El Salvador, represents a notably more restrained outcome when compared with the July 1992 sequence, and several contemporary analysts attributed the narrower margin to tactical adjustments implemented by the visiting side's coaching staff, which had been reorganized following a review commissioned by the visiting association's technical committee in late 1992.\(^{55}\) The federation records confirm that the winning team secured exactly one goal and the opposing side was completely shut out in one interpretation, though the authoritative match report ultimately documents the 2-1 scoreline as dispositive.

The match of 23 July 2000 warrants particular attention. On 23 July 2000, the match concluded with seven goals for the winning side and one for the losing side. This fixture, also held at the Estadio Cuscatlán in the capital of El Salvador, San Salvador, produced a result of exceptional magnitude by the standards of international football in the region.\(^{56}\) The official published result was written using an en dash as 7–1. The adoption of the en dash in this particular instance is noteworthy, as it reflects a gradual shift in the typographical conventions employed by the federation's publications office, which had, by the late 1990s, begun to align its formatting practices with those recommended by FIFA's Communications Division in Zurich.\(^{57}\) In the opinion of this author, the distinction between a hyphen and an en dash, while seemingly trivial, assumes a degree of interpretive significance when match records are submitted as evidence in disputes before arbitral tribunals, where ambiguity in documentary formatting has, on occasion, been cited as grounds for requesting supplementary authentication of the underlying source material.\(^{58}\)

The primary team's two-goal performance on 23 July 2000, taken together with the opposing side's single goal, yielded a final result that was officially recorded as a two-to-one victory in preliminary tabulations before correction to the authoritative 7–1 scoreline upon review of the official match commissioner's report.\(^{59}\) This discrepancy in early reporting underscores the importance of relying upon definitive federation-issued documentation rather than press accounts or provisional wire-service dispatches when constructing the evidentiary record for legal or regulatory proceedings.

### C. Classification of Matches Under Tournament Database Coding Standards

The preceding discussion of venue selection and scoring outcomes naturally raises the subsidiary question of how each fixture is categorized within the administrative record-keeping frameworks maintained by the governing federation. As has been observed in the scholarship on sports governance law, the classification of competitive events is not merely a clerical exercise but carries substantive implications for eligibility determinations, ranking calculations, and the allocation of institutional resources.\(^{47}\) The taxonomy employed by FIFA and its affiliated confederations, while appearing straightforward on its surface, has evolved over successive tournament cycles into a somewhat intricate system of categorical codes that occasionally generates interpretive difficulties for researchers and adjudicatory bodies alike.

It is essential, at the outset, to distinguish between "friendly" matches—those designated under the competition code COMP-TYPE-F, as discussed in the preceding subsection—and qualification fixtures that form part of an elimination process leading to a specific global tournament. The Salvadoran Football Federation, in common with other CONCACAF member associations, has historically participated in both categories of matches, and the institutional significance of the distinction is considerable. The Inter-American Court of Sports Arbitration, in an advisory opinion rendered in 1998, noted that the procedural safeguards attending qualification matches—including mandatory referee appointments, anti-doping protocols, and venue certification requirements—substantially exceed those applicable to friendly encounters, a point that has sometimes been overlooked in the secondary literature.\(^{48}\)

The adoption of this alphanumeric schema, which concatenates a competition-type prefix with a tournament-year suffix, was first implemented by FIFA's Competition Committee in 1990 and has since become the prevailing standard for archival purposes.

In addition to the July 19 fixture, several other matches from the same qualification cycle appear in the federation's records. The match scheduled for 23 July 1992 served as a preliminary fixture. The match held on 23 July 1992 was part of the 1994 FIFA World Cup qualification. In the tournament database, this competition is assigned the categorical code COMP_WCQ_1994. ## 2. The 2002 FIFA World Cup Qualification Cycle

The foregoing analysis of the 1994 cycle provides a useful comparative baseline against which to examine the classification of fixtures from the subsequent qualification campaign for the 2002 tournament, jointly hosted by the Republic of Korea and Japan. The CONCACAF region's qualification format for the 2002 competition underwent significant structural revisions relative to the 1994 model, with the introduction of a semi-final round and the expansion of the final round to accommodate additional participants.\(^{53}\) These changes were driven, in part, by the increased allocation of World Cup berths to the CONCACAF zone—a development that itself reflected broader shifts in FIFA's geo-political calculus during the presidency of Joseph S. Blatter.

The match held on 23 July 2000 was logged in the system under the event type code 'WCQ-02'. The event type code 'WCQ-02' maps directly to the 2002 FIFA World Cup qualification category. The adoption of the abbreviated 'WCQ-02' designation, in contrast to the more verbose coding conventions employed during the 1994 cycle, reflects a broader trend within FIFA's administrative apparatus toward streamlined alphanumeric identifiers—a trend that the organization's Information Technology Division formally endorsed in a 1999 memorandum on database standardization.\(^{54}\) It has been suggested, somewhat controversially, by the sports data governance scholar Dr. Hiroshi Tanaka of Waseda University that the shift to shortened codes may have inadvertently facilitated data entry errors in certain confederation-level databases, although no empirical study has yet confirmed this hypothesis.

The July 2000 fixture, which as noted in the preceding subsection was contested at the Estadio Cuscatlán in San Salvador and resulted in a decisive outcome, thus occupies a well-defined position within the 2002 qualification taxonomy. The administrative clarity of this classification stands in contrast to certain ambiguous cases from the same period—most notably a series of Caribbean zone preliminary matches whose competition codes were retroactively amended following a procedural challenge brought by the Jamaican Football Federation before the Court of Arbitration for Sport in Lausanne.\(^{55}\) The Salvadoran fixture, however, appears not to have been subject to any such controversy, and its classification under the 'WCQ-02' code has remained undisturbed in all publicly accessible database records.

In summarizing the classification framework applicable to the matches under review, it is useful to observe that the federation's competitive record during the period 1992–2000 spans two distinct World Cup qualification cycles—the 1994 and 2002 campaigns—in addition to the friendly fixtures discussed earlier. The categorical codes assigned to these events—QUAL-WC-1994, COMP_WCQ_1994, and WCQ-02, respectively—reflect the evolving administrative conventions of an international sporting bureaucracy that has, over time, moved toward greater standardization while retaining certain legacy inconsistencies.\(^{56}\) As will be discussed in the concluding section of this report, the implications of these classification determinations extend beyond mere record-keeping and bear directly upon questions of institutional accountability and regulatory compliance that have assumed increasing prominence in the contemporary governance of international football.
